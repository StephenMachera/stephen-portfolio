The Monday after we buried my sister, I walked back into school with my head down.

I didn't want to talk to anyone. I didn't want anyone to look at me. I just wanted to sit at the back, get through the day, and go back to the only thing that made sense to me then: hustling.

## Before

By then, I was already carrying more than a child should carry.

My mother was sick, so I worked. Mjengo, odd jobs, whatever paid something. I worked before school, after school, and sometimes instead of school. I was tired all the time. I was also picking up habits that were slowly pulling me down a road that had only one ending.

School had become a place I passed through. I wasn't failing because I couldn't learn. I was failing because my mind was always somewhere else: on food, on money, on home.

And I truly believed nobody noticed. To the world, I was just another boy at the back of the class.

Then I lost my sister. My best sister. And something in me went very quiet.

## The moment

That Monday, Madam Elizabeth, my Kiswahili teacher, called me aside.

My heart dropped. I thought I was in trouble.

But she didn't shout. She said she was sorry for my loss. Then she asked me about my life. Not my marks. Not my homework. *My life.*

I don't think anyone had asked me that in a long time.

So I told her. Everything. The hustling. Home. My mother. My sister. The things I was doing that I knew I shouldn't. It came out slowly at first, then all at once.

She listened to all of it. And when I finished, she didn't pity me. She told me something I have never forgotten: if I stopped the hustles and gave my education everything, she would support me.

No speech. Just a promise, and a choice for me to make.

## What changed

I chose to believe her.

It wasn't easy. Stopping the hustles meant letting go of the only way I knew how to survive. But I didn't have to survive alone anymore, because Madam Elizabeth didn't just keep her promise. She went far beyond it.

She took me in as her own child.

She made sure I had food. She made sure I had clothes. And sometimes, she went even further and supported my mother at home, so that I could sit in class without worrying about what was happening there.

For the first time in a long time, I could just be a student.

So I put everything into my books. Slowly, my marks started to climb. And by the end of primary school, the boy at the back of the class was one of the best students in the whole school.

## What I learned

Back then, I thought nobody saw me. I was wrong.

> There is always someone watching. Keep doing.

You may feel invisible right now: working hard, carrying heavy things, wondering if anyone cares. Someone sees you. A teacher. A neighbour. A stranger. God. Keep doing the right thing, even when it feels like nobody is watching, because one day somebody will stop you in a corridor and ask about your life.

And if you are the teacher, the parent or the adult in someone's life: never underestimate one conversation. Madam Elizabeth only asked me one question. Then she opened her home to me. That is how doors open, one person at a time.

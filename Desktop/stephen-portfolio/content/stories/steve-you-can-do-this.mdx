My family had one plan for me, and I had one plan for myself: I would become a teacher.

I was admitted to study Agricultural Education and Extension. At home, everyone believed teaching was the fastest way to change our story. It was a steady job. It was respect. It was hope. To my family, that course was the game changer.

I carried that hope with me all the way to the University of Embu.

## The "no"

Orientation week went well. I was a first year, excited and a little lost like everyone else.

On the last day, they asked some of us to remain behind for a moment. Then they told us to come back the next day.

The next day, they gave us the news. We could not continue with the course. It needed teachable subjects, and I had failed English and never studied agriculture in high school. They suggested we look at other courses.

Just like that, the plan was gone.

I felt like a failure. I kept thinking about home, about everyone who believed this course would change things. And on top of the pain, I was confused. I truly did not know what to do next.

## Trying to move on

I didn't want to sit and wait, so I made a decision on my own: I would study Biochemistry. I even started attending Biochemistry classes before my transfer was processed. At least I was moving.

But I still had to go through the career office to sort out my course.

## The moment

The person who checked my cluster points that day was Madam Rachael, a university worker in the career office.

She looked at my results. Then she looked closely at my science subjects. And she started asking me questions: what I liked, what I was good at, what I wanted.

Then she suggested something I had never heard of: Computer Science.

I had never heard that name before. I had used a desktop computer once or twice, but by then I couldn't even remember how to switch one on. Computer Science sounded like something meant for other people.

But Madam Rachael had already seen something in my results that I hadn't seen in myself. She looked at me and said:

> "Steve, you can do this."

I believed her. So I said yes.

## What changed

My first year proved her right.

Most of my units were mathematics, and mathematics is what I love most. Suddenly the strange new course didn't feel so strange. It felt like home. Our lecturers were encouraging, and slowly I started saying it to myself too: *I can do this.*

Madam Rachael didn't stop at advice. She connected me with her brother, Chris, and he became my mentor. He guided me, pushed me, and helped me take courses beyond the classroom, like Harvard's CS50. He walked with me as a full mentor, the kind of person everyone needs and very few people get.

The boy who couldn't remember how to boot a computer now builds software for real businesses, and does research in machine learning.

## What I learned

Sometimes the door you were pushing on closes, and it feels like the end. But a closed door can be the beginning of the right road. I didn't choose Computer Science. It found me, through a woman doing her job with care.

Today, I am a Career Peer Advisor at the same university, helping other students find their own path. When a confused first year sits in front of me, I remember that day in the career office. And sometimes, I get to say the words that were once said to me.

If you're standing in front of a closed door right now, don't stop moving. Keep showing up. The right person might be sitting in the next office.

To Madam Rachael: you are a great woman. Thank you for seeing me.

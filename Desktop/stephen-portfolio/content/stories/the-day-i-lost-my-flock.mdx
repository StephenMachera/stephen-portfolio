I used to keep chickens. 125 of them.

I checked on them every day — once in the morning, sometimes again in the evening.
They ate, they moved around, they looked fine. As far as I could tell, everything
was normal.

Then one of them died.

That was the first sign that something was wrong. Not a cough, not a bird standing
alone in a corner that I noticed in time. The first sign I saw was a death.

By the time I understood what was happening, it was spreading through the flock.
In the end I lost about 105 of my 125 birds.

## The part that hurt most

The worst part was not the number. It was finding out that **a cure existed**.

The disease could have been treated. If I had known a few days earlier, most of
those birds would have lived. I wasn't careless — I was there every day. I just
couldn't see what was happening until it was too late to act.

Those birds would have sold for about **KES 150,000**. For a small farmer, that is
not a small loss.

## The question I couldn't stop asking

After that, one question stayed with me:

> If I couldn't catch it with 125 birds, checking every day — how is a farmer with
> 200 or more birds, and other work to do, supposed to catch it?

They can't. Not by looking alone. A person can only check a few times a day. Sickness
doesn't wait for those moments.

## What I did about it

So I started building **Jicho** — Swahili for *eye*.

The idea is simple: cameras watch the birds all day and all night. A computer vision
model looks for the early signs a person would miss — changes in how birds move,
how they stand, how their feathers look. The moment something looks wrong, the
farmer gets an alert on their phone.

Not after the first death. Before it.

I want it to work for real farmers, so it has to be simple — no technical knowledge
needed — and affordable. My target is a setup that costs no more than **KES 30,000**,
far less than what I lost. And I'm testing it on my own poultry, because I know
exactly what's at stake.

## What I'd want you to take from this

You don't need a big failure to start something meaningful. But if one comes, look
closely at it.

My loss came from a gap — between when a problem starts and when a person can see it.
Most painful problems hide in a gap like that. Find the gap, and you've often found
something worth building.

I couldn't save that flock. I'm building Jicho so other farmers don't have to learn
this the way I did.

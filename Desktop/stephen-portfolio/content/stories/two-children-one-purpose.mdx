Four years after I lost my sister, her children lost their father too.

Just like that, two young children were left with nobody. They ended up on the street.

My mother went and took them in. She did everything she could. But by the time I was in my third year of campus, she couldn't support them anymore. She was ready to let them go.

I couldn't let that happen. They were my sister's children.

So I took them.

## A student raising children

I was still in campus. I was still depending on myself for everything: my fees, my food, my rent. And now two children were depending on me.

When I think back, the only word I have for that feeling is *supernatural*. It didn't make sense on paper. A student, with nothing, raising children. But it felt right in a way I can't fully explain, and I felt carried.

That doesn't mean it was easy.

## The long days

At first, every day felt twice as long.

I would wake up already doing the maths. What will they eat today? How will I pay their school fees? Where will their clothes come from? And all of it while I was still figuring out how to feed and pay for myself.

Those first months were hectic. Some nights I lay awake not knowing how the next week would work. But somehow, it always did.

## Learning to be a parent

The kids had grown up on the street, and the street had taught them survival habits. Habits that kept them alive out there, but caused trouble at home and with our neighbours. There were complaints. There were fights.

I had to be firm. I was young, and I was learning to parent while they were learning to trust that they were finally safe. Some days were very hard, for all three of us.

## The day it turned

Then, slowly, things started to change.

The kids began to settle. Then they began to do well in school. Really well.

And the same neighbours who used to come to my door with complaints started saying good things about them. Some even started recommending them to others.

I can't describe how that felt. Those were the children people had given up on. And now people were proud of them.

## What it changed in me

Taking them in changed how I see work.

I needed money, badly. And I realised that the way to earn it wasn't to do the bare minimum. It was to **solve problems** for people. When you fix something real for someone, they value you, and they come back.

So I started building a new mindset: in any work I do, whether waiting tables, cooking or writing code, I stand out. I do it better than it needs to be done. I look for the problem nobody else is solving.

That mindset is how I ended up in the kitchen, then building restaurant systems, then building software for clients. It started with two children who needed to eat.

## What I hope for them

I want these children to stand out in their generation, in whatever they choose to do.

They started life with less than most. But I've seen what they can do when someone believes in them. Someone once believed in me too.

## For you

If you're young and suddenly carrying a family, I know how heavy it is. I know the long nights and the maths that never adds up.

> Don't give up. Sometimes the burden you are carrying is gold.

You may not see it yet. But the weight that's bending you today may be the very thing that is making you strong enough for what's coming.
